\begin{abstract}
Timber harvest schedules computed by linear programming are presented as credible long-term plans, yet they are solved once, as if every future decision could be fixed today. Real planning is sequential: each period, planners re-solve from the realized forest. In his 1991 doctoral thesis, P. J. Daugherty showed that such open-loop plans can be dynamically inconsistent: later planners, re-solving the same model, would not follow them. We formalize the phenomenon with an operational divergence metric and provide an open, reproducible benchmark, reconstructing the thesis case study from public Umpqua National Forest planning documents. Across 432 scenarios (18 initial forests, four discount rates, six harvest-flow policies), inconsistency occurred in 73\% of scenarios with harvest-flow constraints and was largely absent without them at positive discount rates. It occurred at every discount rate, with rate-dependent magnitude, and non-declining yield was the most frequently inconsistent policy. On an all-mature forest, replanning revised the promised non-declining harvest downward over most of the horizon. In four mitigation experiments, declining discount rates and revenue-denominated flow constraints worsened the inconsistency, a rolling-mean flow floor showed that the replanning institution matters, and an iteratively calibrated harvest cap eliminated it, at a lower but credible harvest level.
\end{abstract}
