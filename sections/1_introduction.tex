\section{Introduction}

Linear programming (LP) has been the workhorse of strategic forest planning for half a century. Since the Model~I and Model~II formulations of \citet{johnson1977techniques}, LP harvest-scheduling models, such as the USDA Forest Service's FORPLAN system, have been used to project timber supply, to test harvest-flow policies, and to analyze trade-offs through the shadow prices of an optimal solution. In nearly all such applications, the model is solved once over a multi-decade horizon as an \emph{open-loop} problem: every future decision is chosen at time zero, and the resulting schedule is presented as the plan.

Real planning is sequential: each period, the planner observes the realized forest and solves a new plan of the same form, over a horizon rolled forward. If the remainder of the original plan is not optimal for that new problem, the planner deviates from it, and the announced plan is \emph{dynamically inconsistent}. Its projected harvests, residual growing stock, and shadow prices are then not credible statements about what will happen, and trade-off analyses based on them may misstate the trade-offs that will actually be faced.

Dynamic inconsistency is also a problem between generations. An open-loop plan can raise today's harvest by scheduling harvests that later planners will no longer find worth taking \citep[p.~332]{gunn2007models}: such decisions are selected for a value that the first planner captures and later planners treat as sunk \citep[p.~41]{daugherty1991credibility}---an ``income transfer from future planners to the current planner'' \citep[p.~142]{daugherty1991credibility}; see also \citet{church1999considering} on intergenerational equity in LP-based forest planning. Dynamic inconsistency is a general phenomenon in dynamic economics \citep{strotz1956myopia,kydland1977rules}. For forest planning, P. J. Daugherty showed analytically and numerically in his 1991 PhD thesis that open-loop LP models admit dynamically inconsistent plans, and characterized when and how strongly they arise \citep{daugherty1991credibility}. The thesis remains, to our knowledge, the most complete treatment of the problem, yet it was never published as a journal article. The phenomenon has surfaced under other names: the ``declining even-flow effect'' \citep{mcquillan1986declining}, the ``declining non-declining yield'' \citep[p.~332, citing Daugherty]{gunn2007models}, and the drift between planned and implemented harvest under incoherent hierarchical planning \citep{paradis2013risk}. The field has lacked an open, reproducible implementation of the original analysis.

This paper provides one: an open reproduction of the core of Daugherty's methods and case study, built on the \texttt{ws3} wood-supply model \citep{paradis2026ws3} and public-domain Umpqua planning documents. First, alongside the thesis's volume-inconsistency measure \citep[eq.~5-1]{daugherty1991credibility}, we define a bounded per-period divergence metric with an explicit occurrence criterion. Second, the open package contains the case-study data, the LP formulations, the sequential-replanning simulator, and a new \texttt{ws3} constraint form for the FORPLAN harvest-flow policies (released in v1.1.0a5). Third, we run the full factorial of the thesis's 18 initial forests, 4 discount rates, and 6 harvest-flow policies (432 scenarios; the thesis ran 178 selected combinations, also varying management-intensity and timing choices), and extend the thesis's own tests of carried harvest history and horizon effects \citep[pp.~120, 123]{daugherty1991credibility} to the full grid. Fourth, four extension experiments ask whether alternative formulations mitigate the inconsistency: declining discount rates, a revenue-denominated flow constraint, a rolling-mean flow constraint, and a calibrated harvest cap. The science is Daugherty's; the novelty is the open benchmark and the experiments it enables.
