\section{Background and theory}

\hypertarget{dynamic-inconsistency-and-bellmans-principle}{%
\subsection{Dynamic inconsistency and Bellman's principle}\label{dynamic-inconsistency-and-bellmans-principle}}

Consider a planner who at time zero chooses a sequence of decisions---here, harvest levels by stratum (defined, e.g., by species, management prescription, and age) and period---over a finite horizon of \(T\) periods, so as to maximize an objective (here, net present value, NPV) subject to constraints, including a harvest-flow constraint that links consecutive periods. The open-loop solution is a complete plan: decisions for every period, chosen once by today's decision maker at time zero from the initial forest state.

Now suppose the plan is executed one period at a time by a sequence of planners, each inheriting the forest state realized by her predecessors and each solving the \emph{same} optimization problem from that state over the remaining horizon. Bellman's principle of optimality states that an optimal policy has the property that, whatever the initial state and initial decision, the remaining decisions must constitute an optimal policy with regard to the state resulting from the first decision \citep{bellman1957dynamic}. A plan that satisfies this property is \emph{dynamically consistent}: its tail is optimal for every subproblem it passes through. Each successive planner, re-solving from the realized state, voluntarily continues the plan. A plan that fails the property is \emph{dynamically inconsistent}: at some point the re-solving planner finds the plan's tail suboptimal for the state she has inherited and deviates from it. The period-by-period harvest trajectory of the open-loop solution is, therefore, not a forecast of realized harvests, but a projection under the counterfactual assumption of full precommitment to the time-zero plan.

Formally, let \(x = (x_1, \ldots, x_T)\) be a sequence of decisions, and let the open-loop problem from state \(s_\tau\) over the remaining horizon be \(V_\tau(s_\tau) = \max_{x_\tau, \ldots, x_T} \sum_{t=\tau}^{T} \delta^{\,t-\tau} \, u_t(x_t)\) subject to the model's constraints, where \(\delta\) is the per-period discount factor and $ u_t(x_t)$ denotes the corresponding period-$t$ contribution to the objective. The open-loop plan announced at time zero is \(x^{0} = (x_1^{0}, \ldots, x_T^{0}) = \arg\max V_1(s_1)\). Under sequential replanning, the planner at period \(\tau\) observes the realized state \(s_\tau = f(s_{\tau-1}, x_{\tau-1})\) (the forest left by her predecessor's decision) and re-solves the maximization problem from that state, \(V_\tau(s_\tau)\). The plan \(x^{0}\) is dynamically consistent if its tail is optimal for every subproblem it passes through, i.e., if \((x_\tau^{0}, \ldots, x_T^{0})\) remains a maximizer of \(V_\tau(s_\tau)\) for all \(\tau\); otherwise, some planner deviates and the plan is not followed.

These considerations have practical implications, as long-term forest plans are used to set allowable cuts, to justify regulation of harvest flow, and to price constraints and land uses through shadow prices. If the plan is dynamically inconsistent, the projected harvest levels will not be realized, the projected residual forest will not exist, and the shadow prices will characterize trade-offs for an optimal solution that will not be followed, limiting their validity as measures of trade-offs along the realized trajectory.

\hypertarget{the-open-loop-lp-forest-planning-problem}{%
\subsection{The open-loop LP forest-planning problem}\label{the-open-loop-lp-forest-planning-problem}}

The standard strategic timber harvest-scheduling model is strata-based. The forest is partitioned into development types (strata) defined by attributes such as site, species composition, age, and management prescription. The decision variables allocate stratum area to management activities over a horizon of discrete periods. The classical formulations are those of \citet{johnson1977techniques}. In \emph{Model~I}, each existing stratum is assigned a complete management schedule as a distinct activity, so that the trajectory of every initial cohort is represented explicitly. In \emph{Model~II}, decisions are expressed more compactly as area flows into a smaller set of prescription activities; \emph{Model~III} aggregates further into a network flow over age classes. We refer the reader to \citet[ch.~16]{gunn2007models} for a careful statement of the Model~I/II/III distinction.

Model~II is often described as the more compact formulation because of its smaller variable count \citep{johnson1977techniques}. On realistic problems with many strata, however, \citet{martin2017comparing} found that this advantage can reverse: Model~II's denser constraint matrix made it markedly slower to solve than Model~I.

\citet{daugherty1991credibility} conducted his analysis using a Model~II formulation. The reproduction reported here presents the case study as a Model~I model in \texttt{ws3}, because \texttt{ws3} does not yet compile the Model~II harvest-flow constraints; on the evidence above, the substitution is also computationally favorable. The two formulations are equivalent as network representations only when they encompass the same management alternatives, which implemented models generally do not \citep[pp.~328--329]{gunn2007models}, and our FEIS-based prescription mapping simplifies some of the thesis's treatments (Section~5.5). The behavioral equivalence of the substitution under sequential replanning has therefore not been demonstrated explicitly, and we report it as a limitation.

Two design decisions in the standard formulation are important for this analysis. First, the objective is NPV: timber values and costs in each period are weighted by a discount factor, so positive net revenue is preferred early and net costs late, all else being equal. A negatively valued stratum---in the case study, the high-elevation mountain-hemlock stands---is therefore best deferred: in the thesis's model it should enter a solution only because harvesting it relaxes binding harvest-flow constraints \citep[pp.~72, 76]{daugherty1991credibility}, and plans tend to schedule such expensive harvests late, where discounting makes them cheap \citep[p.~332]{gunn2007models}. Second, harvest-flow constraints, typically even-flow or bounded-deviation constraints on total volume between consecutive periods, create a direct link between decisions in different periods. The economics of such harvest-flow constraints---the even-flow constraint and the allowable-cut effect it enables---have a long critical literature \citep{thompson1966traditional,schweitzer1972allowable,luckert1995allowable,hegan2000economic}. Even without such a link, periods are connected through the forest state: today's harvest determines the growing stock and age structure from which every future subproblem starts. That link alone need not cause inconsistency---in the thesis, removing all harvest-flow constraints from McQuillan's example restored a consistent plan \citep[p.~25]{daugherty1991credibility}---although the thesis also reports four inconsistent runs without flow constraints \citep[p.~123]{daugherty1991credibility}, and in our grid the unconstrained control diverges at 0--2\%, where the flat objective admits near-tied optima and the objective-gap diagnostic shows the announced tail to be largely still optimal (Section~5.2). A harvest-flow constraint adds a second, direct link: it ties each period's harvest \emph{level} to the previous one, so that the current decision constrains every future subproblem beyond its effect on the forest state.

Writing \(H_t\) for the total harvest volume in period \(t\), without distinguishing among strata, the open-loop problem maximizes discounted net revenue subject to a harvest-flow constraint linking consecutive periods:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:openloop}
    \begin{align}
        \max_{H_1, \ldots, H_T \geq 0} \quad
            & \sum_{t=1}^{T} \delta^{\,t} r_t H_t
            \label{eq:openloop_obj} \\
        \text{s.t.} \quad
            & (1-\alpha) H_t \leq H_{t+1} \leq (1+\beta) H_t,
            \quad t=1,\ldots,T-1.
            \label{eq:openloop_flow}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where \(r_t\) is net revenue per unit volume in period \(t\) and \(\delta = (1+i)^{-1}\) is the discount factor for the annual discount rate $i$ (the thesis's ``interest rate''). The harvest-flow constraint is the classic FORPLAN bounded-deviation ("sequential flow") form \citep[eq.~3-4, 3-5]{daugherty1991credibility}: \(\alpha\) is the maximum allowed fractional \emph{decrease} and \(\beta\) the maximum allowed fractional \emph{increase} between consecutive periods. We implemented it in \texttt{ws3} as a new even-flow constraint form with consecutive-period anchoring and separate decrease and increase tolerances. Its special cases span the standard harvest-flow policies: non-declining yield (\(\alpha = 0\), no increase bound; the yield may not fall), bounded decline (\(\alpha > 0\), no increase bound), and symmetric bounded deviation (\(\alpha = \beta > 0\)).

\hypertarget{daughertys-thesis}{%
\subsection{Dynamic inconsistency of open-loop forest plans}\label{daughertys-thesis}}

\citet{daugherty1991credibility} posed the consistency question directly---are the plans produced by LP-based forest-planning models, solved open-loop, plans that a sequence of future planners operating the same model would actually follow?---and tied a plan's credibility to it: the larger the inconsistency, the less credible the plan. The thesis (i) formalized dynamic consistency for LP forest-planning models, (ii) derived conditions under which open-loop optimal plans fail the consistency requirements, and (iii) demonstrated the phenomenon numerically by iterative LP simulation of sequential replanning on an empirically based case study---a model built from the Umpqua National Forest planning data \citep{usda1987umpqua} and applied to 18 constructed initial forest conditions---across a designed experiment varying the initial forest condition, the harvest policy, the discount rate, and the range of management and timing choices. The thesis found dynamic inconsistency to be the rule rather than the exception: 172 of its 178 simulations were inconsistent \citep[p.~92]{daugherty1991credibility}. It traced the inconsistency to decisions selected for their \emph{cut effect value}---their value to the current planner through their effect, via binding harvest-flow constraints, on the current harvest, which the next planner treats as sunk \citep[p.~41]{daugherty1991credibility}. It found inconsistency with every initial forest condition and harvest-flow constraint it tested, including young-growth forests without over-mature stands, and with or without negatively valued strata \citep[pp.~92, 122]{daugherty1991credibility}, and it named the open-loop solution method itself ``the real culprit'' \citep[p.~136]{daugherty1991credibility}. \citet[pp.~331--332]{gunn2007models}, citing \citet{daugherty1991credibility}, summarizes the practical symptom memorably as the ``declining non-declining yield'': a plan that promises a non-declining harvest trajectory while the sequential re-optimization it invites systematically revises the attainable harvest downward.
