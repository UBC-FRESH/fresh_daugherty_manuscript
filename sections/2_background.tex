\section{Background and theory}

\hypertarget{dynamic-inconsistency-and-bellmans-principle}{%
\subsection{Dynamic inconsistency and Bellman's principle}\label{dynamic-inconsistency-and-bellmans-principle}}

Consider a planner who at time zero chooses harvest levels by stratum and period over \(T\) periods to maximize net present value (NPV), subject to constraints that include a harvest-flow constraint linking consecutive periods. The open-loop solution is a complete plan, chosen once from the initial forest state. Suppose the plan is executed one period at a time by a sequence of planners, each inheriting the forest state left by her predecessors. Bellman's principle of optimality states that an optimal policy has the property that, whatever the initial state and initial decision, the remaining decisions must constitute an optimal policy with regard to the state resulting from the first decision \citep{bellman1957dynamic}. If each planner solved exactly the remainder of the original problem, she would therefore continue the plan, up to ties among alternate optima. Sequential forest planning does not work this way: each new plan covers a full horizon rolled forward from the realized state, and its flow constraints link only its own periods, so the harvest already realized is sunk \citep[pp.~41, 82]{daugherty1991credibility}. The open-loop plan is \emph{dynamically consistent} under this replanning institution if each successive planner voluntarily continues it, and \emph{dynamically inconsistent} otherwise; its trajectory is then a projection under full precommitment, not a forecast.

Formally, the planner at period \(\tau\) solves \(V_\tau(s_\tau) = \max \sum_{t=\tau}^{\tau+T-1} \delta^{\,t-\tau} \, u_t(x_t)\) from the realized state \(s_\tau\), subject to the model's constraints over periods \(\tau, \ldots, \tau+T-1\), where \(x_t\) are the period-\(t\) decisions, \(\delta\) is the per-period discount factor, and \(u_t\) is the period-\(t\) contribution to the objective, and she implements her first decision \(x_\tau^{\tau}\). The plan \(x^{0} = \arg\max V_1(s_1)\) is dynamically consistent if \(x_\tau^{\tau} = x_\tau^{0}\) for all \(\tau \leq T\), up to alternate optima. Keeping the terminal date \(T\) and constraining each replan's first harvest by the realized previous harvest turns \(V_\tau\) into the exact tail problem, for which Bellman's principle makes \(x^{0}\) consistent; we simulate both institutions (Section~\ref{the-sequential-replanning-simulator}).

Rolling-horizon replanning is also a standard response to uncertainty such as fire, replacing uncertain losses by their mean and revising the plan regularly \citep[\citealp{gunn1991some}; see][p.~333]{gunn2007models}; fire losses can also be represented by expected values \citep{reed1986optimal} or treated by stochastic programming \citep{gassmann1989optimal,boychuk1996multistage}. The problem studied here is deterministic, so any deviation arises from the replanning institution, not from new information. Rolling a finite horizon forward also exposes end effects \citep{grinold1983model}.

\hypertarget{the-open-loop-lp-forest-planning-problem}{%
\subsection{The open-loop LP forest-planning problem}\label{the-open-loop-lp-forest-planning-problem}}

The standard strategic harvest-scheduling model partitions the forest into strata (development types defined by site, species, age, and prescription) and allocates stratum area to management activities over discrete periods \citep{johnson1977techniques}. Two features matter here. First, with an NPV objective, net revenue is preferred early and net costs late, so a negatively valued stratum---in the case study, the high-elevation mountain-hemlock stands---should enter a solution only because harvesting it relaxes binding flow constraints \citep[pp.~72, 76]{daugherty1991credibility}, and plans tend to schedule such harvests late \citep[p.~332]{gunn2007models}. Second, harvest-flow constraints link the harvest levels of consecutive periods directly. Their economics have long been questioned: the regulated, even-flow forest is at odds with economic criteria \citep{thompson1966traditional}, and the allowable-cut effect \citep{schweitzer1972allowable}, an immediate increase in today's allowable cut credited to expected future yield gains, has proved a weak incentive for silvicultural investment \citep{luckert1995allowable,hegan2000economic}. Periods are also connected through the forest state, but that link alone need not cause inconsistency: removing all flow constraints from McQuillan's example restored a consistent plan \citep[p.~25]{daugherty1991credibility}.

Writing \(H_t\) for the total harvest volume in period \(t\), the open-loop problem is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:openloop}
    \begin{align}
        \max_{H_1, \ldots, H_T \geq 0} \quad
            & \sum_{t=1}^{T} \delta^{\,t} r_t H_t
            \label{eq:openloop_obj} \\
        \text{s.t.} \quad
            & (1-\alpha) H_t \leq H_{t+1} \leq (1+\beta) H_t,
            \quad t=1,\ldots,T-1,
            \label{eq:openloop_flow}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where \(r_t\) is net revenue per unit volume and \(\delta = (1+i)^{-10}\) is the per-period (decadal) discount factor for the annual rate $i$ (the thesis's ``interest rate''; it discounts to mid-period, \(\delta^{\,t-0.5}\), which rescales the objective by a constant; \citealp[p.~58]{daugherty1991credibility}). Constraint~\eqref{eq:openloop_flow} is the FORPLAN bounded-deviation (``sequential flow'') form \citep[eq.~3-4, 3-5]{daugherty1991credibility}, with maximum fractional decrease \(\alpha\) and increase \(\beta\) between consecutive periods. Its special cases are non-declining yield (\(\alpha = 0\), no increase bound), bounded decline (\(\alpha > 0\), no increase bound), and symmetric bounded deviation (\(\alpha = \beta > 0\)).

\hypertarget{daughertys-thesis}{%
\subsection{Dynamic inconsistency of open-loop forest plans}\label{daughertys-thesis}}

\citet{daugherty1991credibility} asked whether plans from open-loop LP forest-planning models are plans that a sequence of future planners would follow, and tied a plan's credibility to the answer. The thesis formalized dynamic consistency for these models, derived conditions under which open-loop optimal plans fail it, and simulated sequential replanning on a case study built from the Umpqua National Forest planning data \citep{usda1987umpqua}, varying the initial forest (18 landbases), the harvest policy, the discount rate, and the range of management and timing choices. Inconsistency was the rule: 172 of 178 simulations were inconsistent \citep[p.~92]{daugherty1991credibility}, on every initial forest with binding flow constraints, under every flow policy, and with or without negatively valued strata \citep[pp.~92, 122]{daugherty1991credibility}. The thesis traced it to decisions selected for their \emph{cut effect value}---their value to the current planner through their effect, via binding flow constraints, on the current harvest, which the next planner treats as sunk \citep[p.~41]{daugherty1991credibility}. Carrying the previous harvest into each later plan largely removed it (volume inconsistency 1.9\% on average), evidence that it stems from the cut effect rather than horizon effects \citep[pp.~120, 123]{daugherty1991credibility}, and the thesis named the open-loop solution method ``the real culprit'' \citep[p.~136]{daugherty1991credibility}.
