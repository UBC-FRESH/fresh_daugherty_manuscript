\section{Methods}

\hypertarget{case-study-the-umpqua-national-forest-forplan-model}{%
\subsection{Case study: Umpqua National Forest FORPLAN model}\label{case-study-the-umpqua-national-forest-forplan-model}}

The case study reconstructs the thesis's Umpqua National Forest model in \texttt{ws3}. The forest is stratified into four ecoclasses \citep[Table~5.1]{daugherty1991credibility}: \textit{CH-CW} (moist, warm sites; Douglas-fir and western hemlock), \textit{CD-CP} (hot, dry sites; Douglas-fir), \textit{CR-CF} (Shasta red fir and Douglas-fir), and \textit{CM-CE} (high-elevation sites; mountain hemlock and lodgepole pine). Seven silvicultural prescriptions, from natural regeneration to planting with vegetation management, thinning, and fertilization, define the young-growth strata, each with ecoclass-specific rotation ages \citep[Table~5.2]{daugherty1991credibility}. Unlike the thesis, in which a regenerated stand could take any prescription \citep[pp.~28--29, 74]{daugherty1991credibility}, a harvested stand here regenerates under a fixed prescription, and the objective has no treatment costs or commercial thinnings. The thesis used a Model~II formulation; because \texttt{ws3} does not yet compile Model~II flow constraints, we use Model~I. The two are equivalent only when they encompass the same management alternatives \citep[pp.~328--329]{gunn2007models}: with matched prescription sets they reach near-identical optima, but a restricted Model~I prescription set lowered the optimum by up to about 5\% \citep{martin2017comparing}. Our Model~I, with fixed regeneration, is of the restricted kind. The horizon is 15 ten-year periods; the objective is NPV at 4\% (varied in the experiments), with real net values per unit volume escalating at 1\% per year for 50 years. Volumes are reported in cubic metres (the model works in thousand cubic feet, MCF; 1~MCF = \SI{28.3}{\metre\cubed}). Landbase~1, the focus of the examples, consists of the thesis's five mature vegetation types (four financially over-mature types and the negatively valued CM-CE type).

\hypertarget{case-study-data}{%
\subsection{Case-study data}\label{case-study-data}}

The data come from public-domain Umpqua planning documents: the 1987 Draft Environmental Impact Statement that Daugherty used \citep{usda1987umpqua} and the 1990 Final Environmental Impact Statement (FEIS), whose Appendix~B is the FORPLAN analysis volume \citep{usda1990umpqua}; the reconstructed data are published in \citet{freshdaugherty}. Yield curves by ecoclass and prescription come from FEIS Appendix~B (DFSIM managed yields adjusted for operational falldown; mountain-hemlock yields from Johnson's site equations); the tables start at 55--175 years, and younger volumes follow each curve's calibrated Chapman--Richards shape scaled to the first tabled value. Net values come from the FEIS timber-value derivations (Tables~B-65 and B-66) less per-ecoclass access costs, which make CM-CE negatively valued; because the whole net value escalates, CM-CE grows more negative over time. The thesis escalated stumpage, with hauling and road costs uniform across ecoclasses \citep[p.~74]{daugherty1991credibility}, and its CM-CE reaches a small positive value in period 15 \citep[p.~76]{daugherty1991credibility}.

The 1987 and 1990 yield tables agree. Mature-type volumes are calibrated so that the model's discounted value of a period-1 harvest equals the thesis's Table~5.4 (period-2 values are 0.87--0.93 of Table~5.4 and 1.15 for CM-CE, because volumes are flat over age); the managed prescriptions reproduce the signs of Table~5.3's values, but their highest-PNV rotations are the shortest the thesis permits for every productive prescription, 0--40 years shorter than Table~5.3's (40 years without the thesis's ranges, on extrapolated young-age yields), and the longest for CM-CE. Provenance and anchor tables are in the repository (\path{supplementary/01-case-study-data.md}).

\subsection{The sequential-replanning simulator}\label{the-sequential-replanning-simulator}}

Following the thesis's iterative LP simulation, a sequential-replanning simulator applies each plan's first-period decision, advances the forest, and re-solves from the realized state (Figure~\ref{fig:sequential-replanning}).

Two modeling decisions bear on the interpretation. First, the replanning institution. In the core design, each replan covers a full 15-period horizon rolled forward from the realized state (\emph{rolling} horizon) with a fresh flow constraint that links only its own periods (\emph{reset} flow history), as in the thesis \citep[pp.~82, 119--120]{daugherty1991credibility} and FORPLAN practice. To separate the two departures from the exact tail problem (Section~\ref{dynamic-inconsistency-and-bellmans-principle}), we rerun the grid under all four combinations of a rolling or \emph{fixed} horizon (the original terminal date) and a reset or \emph{carried} flow history (the flow constraint also links each replan's first harvest to the realized previous harvest; where this is infeasible, it is loosened by the smallest amount that restores feasibility, and the loosening is recorded). Fixed horizon with carried history is the exact tail problem. In a null test, replanning under it reproduces the open-loop plan, with every period's realized harvest within 0.1\% of the planned harvest, in seven tested combinations of landbase, policy, and rate, which also confirms that rebuilding the model from the realized state preserves the state the replanner needs.

Second, because LP optima can be non-unique, a divergence could reflect an alternate optimum rather than inconsistency (the thesis found few alternative optima among its first-period nonbasic variables; \citealp[p.~124]{daugherty1991credibility}). Our \emph{objective-gap diagnostic} solves each replan freely and again with the period-1 harvest held within 1\% of the announced value; if the free objective exceeds the tail-fixed objective by more than \num{e-4}\% of its value, or the announced value is infeasible, the announced plan is strictly suboptimal or unimplementable, not one of several optima. We also report the gap relative to the optimal objective.

The inconsistency measure compares the open-loop projection \(p = (p_1, \ldots, p_T)\) of harvest volume with the realized replanned trajectory \(r\), period by period:
\begin{equation}
d_t = \frac{|p_t - r_t|}{\max(|p_t|, |r_t|, \varepsilon)},
\label{eq:metric}
\end{equation}
where \(\varepsilon\) (\num{e-6} of the larger mean harvest) makes a period without harvest in either trajectory score zero. The metric is dimensionless and bounded in \([0, 1]\). A scenario's \emph{magnitude} is the mean \(\bar d\) of \(d_t\) over the thesis's observation window, periods 2--11 \citep[p.~83]{daugherty1991credibility}, our headline basis (\(d_1 = 0\) by construction); we also report it over all 15 periods. A scenario is inconsistent when \(\bar d\) exceeds 5\% (2--10\% as a sensitivity). We also report the thesis's volume inconsistency, the sum over periods 2--11 of absolute deviations from the first planner's volumes divided by that planner's volume over the same periods \citep[eq.~5-1]{daugherty1991credibility}, which weights periods by announced volume. Both measures concern aggregate harvest, not the stand-level decisions on which consistency is defined; the thesis's decision-level acre inconsistency was larger (44.6\% on average) and only weakly related to volume inconsistency \citep[\(R^2 = 0.40\);][pp.~93--94]{daugherty1991credibility}, and is not reproduced here.

\hypertarget{experiment-design}{%
\subsection{Scenario design}\label{experiment-design}}

The scenario grid crosses three of the thesis's factors in full: initial forest (landbase), harvest-flow policy, and discount rate. The thesis ran 178 selected combinations of these and of the ranges of management and timing choices, which we do not vary; it varied the rate only under non-declining yield and ran the other policies only at 4\% \citep[Tables~5.8, 6.1]{daugherty1991credibility}. All eighteen landbases \citep[Table~5.5]{daugherty1991credibility} are reconstructed from the thesis's descriptions (p.~79; assumptions in the repository): all-mature (1, and 2 without CM-CE); landbase 1 or 2 after 40 or 70 years of area-control harvest at a 100- or 70-year rotation (3--8); intensively managed young growth with equal (9) or irregular (10) age classes; and randomly generated young growth (11--18; one seed-fixed draw each, four further draws as a sensitivity check). Landbases 9--18 contain no CM-CE. The six policies \citep[Table~5.6]{daugherty1991credibility} are no flow constraint (\scen{NHF}, the flow-unconstrained control), non-declining yield (\scen{NDY}), bounded decline of 10\% or 20\% (\scen{\textminus 10\%}, \scen{\textminus 20\%}), and symmetric bounded deviation of 10\% or 20\% (\scen{\textpm 10\%}, \scen{\textpm 20\%}); the rates are 0\%, 2\%, 4\%, and 6\%.

As in the thesis, every flow-constrained plan and replan ends with two terminal constraints that limit horizon effects: the final-period standing volume must be at least 80\% of the average inventory of the forest regulated under the regeneration prescriptions, and the final-period harvest at most 120\% of that forest's long-term sustained yield, both at the thesis's highest-PNV rotations (\citealp[p.~77, Table~5.3]{daugherty1991credibility}; a sensitivity run uses the model's own, Section~\ref{case-study-data}). As in the thesis, they are omitted in the flow-unconstrained runs \citep[p.~80]{daugherty1991credibility}; we also omit them in E2. The ending-inventory coefficients are \texttt{ws3}'s inventory of each column's state after its final action. Our headline basis remains the thesis's observation window.

\hypertarget{extensions-four-mitigation-tests}{%
\subsection{Extensions: four mitigation experiments}\label{extensions-four-mitigation-tests}}

Four extension experiments (E1--E4) each replace one ingredient of the model and are scored like the scenario grid, against it as control (details in \path{supplementary/05}--\texttt{08}).

\textbf{E1---declining discount rates.} The constant rate is replaced by linear declines (4\% or 6\% to 0\% over the horizon) or inverse-j paths (4\% for one or two periods, then halving each period), as advocated for long horizons \citep{weitzman2001gamma,hepburn2007recent}. Each replanning planner applies the schedule from her own present, as a planner re-running such a model would; such preferences are themselves time-inconsistent \citep{strotz1956myopia}, so E1 tests this practice, not calendar-fixed declining rates.

\textbf{E2---calibrated harvest cap.} The flow constraint is replaced by a per-period cap \(H_t \leq \mathrm{cap}\), lowered by bisection until the realized replanned trajectory is even (trend within 1\% of the mean per period, fluctuation and coefficient of variation within 5\%), assuming that a lower cap can only flatten it. This operationalizes, with an upper bound only, the thesis's proposal to replace sequential flow constraints with capacity bounds that persist when the plan is revised \citep[pp.~134--135, 137]{daugherty1991credibility}.

\textbf{E3---revenue-denominated flow.} The six policies constrain undiscounted net revenue instead of volume; because harvesting the negatively valued CM-CE stratum lowers revenue, it should no longer serve as filler under a revenue floor.

\textbf{E4---rolling-mean \scen{NDY}.} Each period's harvest is floored at the mean of the previous \(k \in \{2, 3\}\) periods, with \emph{within-plan} windows (as in the core design) or \emph{realized-history} windows that reach back into realized harvests, a rolling-mean analogue of the thesis's carried-history test \citep[p.~120]{daugherty1991credibility}; infeasible floors are loosened minimally and recorded.
