\section{Discussion}

\hypertarget{what-drives-the-inconsistency}{%
\subsection{Drivers of inconsistency}\label{what-drives-the-inconsistency}}

The reproduction separates the ingredients of the phenomenon. The harvest-flow constraint is the operative between-period link: without it, the announced plan's tail remains largely optimal at each replan; with it, the re-solving planner either improves on the announced tail or cannot implement it from the realized state, and the most restrictive policy, non-declining yield, is the most frequently inconsistent. The disequilibrium forest structure provides the conditions for this inconsistency. The extensions isolate the mechanism further (Section~4, Table~\ref{tab:extensions}). E3 shows that negatively valued CM-CE strata are a marker rather than a necessary condition: under a revenue-denominated floor their harvest is excluded from the projected solution, yet the announced plan is still not followed. E4 shows that the result is robust to the implementation of non-declining yield, although anchoring the constraint to realized harvest reduces the divergence at the cost of frequent relaxations. Together, these results identify the interaction between a binding inter-period flow constraint, the disequilibrium initial forest structure, and its treatment under sequential replanning as the central mechanism.

\hypertarget{the-discount-rate-nuance}{%
\subsection{Role of discount rates}\label{the-discount-rate-nuance}}

One finding deserves emphasis because it is easy to misstate. A plausible hypothesis is that the interaction between a discounted objective and an even-flow constraint causes the violation of Bellman's principle, so that lowering the rate could restore consistency. Yet flow-constrained plans are inconsistent at every rate tested (0\%, 2\%, 4\%, and 6\%). The resolution is in the thesis itself \citep[ch.~3, pp.~58--60]{daugherty1991credibility}: constant-rate discounting has the form that \citet{strotz1956myopia} showed to be required for consistent planning, so the discount factor, although it enters every term of the consistency conditions, does not by itself create inconsistency, and a positive rate is not required for inconsistency to occur. Finding inconsistency at every rate reproduces this counter-intuitive result on an independent implementation. Discounting nonetheless shapes \emph{which} plan is chosen and the \emph{magnitude} of the divergence, which is markedly larger at 0\% than at 2--6\%. This dependence is new: the thesis varied the rate only under non-declining yield, found every such run inconsistent, and found no relationship between the rate and the magnitude of inconsistency \citep[pp.~92, 113]{daugherty1991credibility}. At 0\%, the flat objective also admits near-tied optima, so the divergence metric flags the unconstrained control even though the objective-gap diagnostic shows its announced tail to be still optimal; at positive rates, the flow-constraint divergence is genuine. Extension E1 adds a second mechanism: declining schedules raise occurrence to 98--100\% and, because each replanning planner re-applies the schedule from her own present, introduce the preference-level time inconsistency described by \citet{strotz1956myopia}, which makes even the unconstrained control inconsistent. Neither lowering a constant rate nor adopting a declining schedule therefore restores plan credibility.

\hypertarget{implications-for-the-literature}{%
\subsection{Implications for the literature}\label{implications-for-the-literature}}

These findings matter for open-loop LP forest-planning studies and for planning practice that relies on single-solve strategic models with inter-period harvest-flow constraints. Applied to a disequilibrium initial forest, such models may project harvest and growing-stock trajectories, and shadow prices, for a plan that is not maintained under sequential re-optimization, so trade-off analyses based on them may differ from those under sequential implementation; the ``declining non-declining yield'' \citep[pp.~331--332]{gunn2007models} is this discrepancy expressed in harvest terms. The same mechanism offers a theoretical explanation for the systematic divergence between planned and implemented harvest that \citet{paradis2013risk} found under incoherent strategic and operational planning. Because the original analysis has received limited direct attention, an open and reproducible implementation makes it available for evaluation and application in forest-planning research.

\hypertarget{remedies}{%
\subsection{Remedies}\label{remedies}}

Three families of remedies follow from the thesis, the subsequent literature, and our results; the thesis itself proposed the first and rejected the second. First, \emph{consistent formulations}: the optimization problem can be formulated to account explicitly for sequential decision-making, yielding subgame-perfect or feedback solutions in which each period's decision is optimal given the state and the continuation policy. The thesis proposes such a formulation and a sequential-recursive method to solve it, without computing consistent solutions \citep[pp.~136--137]{daugherty1991credibility}. Second, \emph{credible precommitment}: inconsistency arises because future planners are free to re-solve. The thesis judged precommitment not viable, because planners need the flexibility to respond to changing conditions \citep[p.~133]{daugherty1991credibility}; narrower institutional commitments, for example regeneration mandates attached to harvest, change the future optimization problem and may render announced plans dynamically consistent \citep[cf.][]{bogle2015protecting}. Third, \emph{receding-horizon practice with consistent reporting}: where plans are implemented by sequential replanning, plan documents could report replanned trajectories, such as those of the simulator used here, alongside or instead of the open-loop projection.

The extensions add a fourth, operational remedy and qualify the role of precommitment. E2 replaces the inter-period flow constraint with a calibrated per-period harvest cap and so removes the dynamic coupling: it finds the highest even harvest level that remains feasible under sequential replanning, eliminating inconsistency here at about 8\% below the \scen{NDY} plan's projected level. The difference quantifies the harvest reduction needed for a credible even flow and gives an operational reading of the allowable-cut effect. Conversely, E4's realized-history formulation shows that binding institutional rules depend on the evolving forest state: anchoring the flow constraint to realized harvest mitigates divergence but requires relaxation in about one fifth to one third of replanning periods. Stronger intertemporal commitment can therefore improve consistency but cannot guarantee feasibility.

\hypertarget{limitations}{%
\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}}

Several limitations define the scope of these claims. First, data vintage: the case study is built from the 1990 FEIS and cross-checked against the 1987 DEIS that Daugherty used; the two vintages' yield tables agree, but the thesis's base-case schedule cannot be matched period by period because its realized schedule is not reported. Validation therefore relies on the thesis's printed anchors (Section~\ref{case-study-data}), and the exact optimal-rotation ordering is only partially reproduced because the FEIS prescription mapping simplifies some treatments. Second, the reproduction uses Model~I where the thesis used Model~II, because \texttt{ws3} does not yet compile Model~II flow constraints; their behavioral equivalence under sequential replanning remains to be demonstrated. Third, the thesis's ending-period constraints are implemented but omitted from the reported scenarios, because with the corrected data the model shows no horizon-end liquidation for them to prevent. Fourth, the simulator uses a rolling (receding) horizon; a shrinking-horizon variant (the pure Bellman tail over a fixed terminal date) shows the same qualitative phenomenon at smaller magnitude (landbase~1, \scen{NDY}, 4\%: mean relative divergence 0.050 vs 0.074; both inconsistent). Fifth, this is a single case study: the 73\% occurrence is a property of this scenario grid, not an estimate of prevalence. Sixth, the extensions are additive experiments against the unchanged core control: the E2 caps depend mildly on the recorded even-flow thresholds, E4's rolling-mean rows rely on pinned \texttt{ws3} constraint internals (regression-tested to reduce exactly to \scen{NDY}), and E1 indexes discount paths by each subproblem's own present, so a calendar-absolute reading of declining schedules remains untested.
