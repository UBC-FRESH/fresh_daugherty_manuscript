\section{Discussion}

\hypertarget{what-drives-the-inconsistency}{%
\subsection{Drivers of inconsistency}\label{what-drives-the-inconsistency}}

The reproduction separates the ingredients of the phenomenon. When each replan solves the exact remainder of the original problem, the plan is followed, as Bellman's principle requires; this verifies the simulator rather than adding a finding. Inconsistency arises from the replanning institution of the thesis and of FORPLAN practice, in which each new plan rolls the horizon forward and links only its own periods by the flow constraint: the re-solving planner, no longer bound to the harvest already realized, improves on the announced plan or cannot implement it. At positive rates, carrying the flow history matters more than fixing the horizon (6 vs 58 of 270 scenarios remain inconsistent; Table~\ref{tab:institutions}), as the thesis's attribution of inconsistency to the cut effect rather than to horizon effects implies \citep[pp.~120, 123]{daugherty1991credibility}. Part of what the core design counts as inconsistency is nevertheless a horizon effect: fixing the terminal date alone lowers occurrence from 49\% to 27\%. Terminal constraints, imposed here as in the thesis, limit end-of-plan liquidation, and the thesis found the remaining horizon effects small \citep[pp.~119, 123]{daugherty1991credibility}; but each rolling replan has its own, later end, so our core estimate overstates the cut effect alone. The initial forest modulates the magnitude, and negatively valued CM-CE strata contribute without being necessary. The central mechanism is thus the one the thesis identified: binding inter-period flow constraints that are ``not rolled forward completely'' when the plan is revised \citep[p.~134]{daugherty1991credibility}.

\hypertarget{the-discount-rate-nuance}{%
\subsection{Role of discount rates}\label{the-discount-rate-nuance}}

A plausible hypothesis is that discounting combined with an even-flow constraint causes inconsistency, so that lowering the rate could restore consistency. Yet flow-constrained plans are inconsistent at every rate tested. The resolution is in the thesis itself \citep[ch.~3, pp.~58--60]{daugherty1991credibility}: constant-rate discounting has the form that \citet{strotz1956myopia} showed to be required for consistent planning, so the discount factor does not by itself create inconsistency. Discounting nonetheless shapes \emph{which} plan is chosen and the \emph{magnitude} of the divergence, which is about four times larger at 0\% than at 2--6\%, whereas occurrence varies little among the positive rates (29--38\%). The thesis found no relationship between the rate and the magnitude of inconsistency in its 40 non-declining-yield runs \citep[p.~113]{daugherty1991credibility}, although its regression over all runs gave the rate a positive coefficient \citep[p.~96]{daugherty1991credibility}; our 0\% result differs, possibly because a nearly flat objective is most exposed to the rolling-horizon effect discussed next. At 0--2\%, the flow-unconstrained control also diverges, but only under a rolling horizon (Table~\ref{tab:institutions}): this low-rate divergence appears to reflect horizon extension rather than ties (we have not tested why a flatter objective is more sensitive to it). The thesis ran its flow-unconstrained control without terminal constraints and found it consistent in all but four young-growth runs \citep[pp.~80, 123]{daugherty1991credibility}. Declining schedules re-applied by each planner (E1) mostly raise occurrence, to as much as 100\%, and add preference-level time inconsistency \citep{strotz1956myopia}. No constant or declining rate tested restores plan credibility.

\hypertarget{implications-for-the-literature}{%
\subsection{Implications for the literature}\label{implications-for-the-literature}}

Open-loop LP studies and single-solve strategic plans with inter-period harvest-flow constraints may project harvest and growing-stock trajectories, and shadow prices, for a plan that is not maintained under sequential re-optimization, so trade-off analyses based on them may differ from those under sequential implementation; the ``declining non-declining yield'' \citep[p.~332]{gunn2007models} is this discrepancy expressed in harvest terms. The same mechanism may contribute to the systematic divergence between planned and implemented harvest that \citet{paradis2013risk} found under incoherent strategic and operational planning; whether it does remains to be tested. In their volume-maximizing model, replanning followed a perfectly implemented plan until a critical period, but with imperfect implementation the replanned harvest declined steadily, a declining non-declining yield driven by implementation error; they noted that the documented cases all involved profit-maximizing objectives \citep{paradis2013risk}. The cut effect, by contrast, requires that harvests differ in value, which suggests that the objective, not only the replanning institution, conditions this source of the phenomenon. Discounting is not needed, however: the largest shortfalls in our grid occur at a zero rate, so the late scheduling of costly harvests that \citet[p.~332]{gunn2007models} describes cannot be the whole explanation.

\hypertarget{remedies}{%
\subsection{Remedies}\label{remedies}}

Three families of remedies follow; the thesis proposed the first and rejected the second. First, \emph{consistent formulations}: the problem can be formulated to account explicitly for sequential decision-making, yielding subgame-perfect or feedback solutions in which each period's decision is optimal given the state and the continuation policy; the thesis proposes such a formulation and a sequential-recursive solution method, without computing consistent solutions \citep[pp.~136--137]{daugherty1991credibility}. \citet{ludwig1993forest} had computed one for an age-structured forest: dynamic programming over a discretized age-class state space gave a stationary feedback policy, with harvest-flow constraints replaced by a penalty on falldown and the previous harvest carried as a state variable, so that the flow concern travels with the plan, as in our carried-history institution. Second, \emph{credible precommitment}: the thesis judged general precommitment not viable, because planners need the flexibility to respond to changing conditions, but it identified legal requirements, such as the obligation to regenerate harvested areas, as credible precommitments that limit inconsistency \citep[p.~133]{daugherty1991credibility}; such commitments change the future optimization problem \citep[cf.][]{bogle2015protecting}. Third, \emph{consistent reporting}: plan documents could report replanned trajectories, such as those simulated here, alongside or instead of the open-loop projection.

The extensions test a fourth remedy, which the thesis proposed \citep[pp.~134--135]{daugherty1991credibility}, and qualify the role of precommitment. E2 replaces the inter-period flow constraint with a calibrated per-period harvest cap, removing the explicit dynamic coupling, although implicit links through the forest state remain, as the thesis noted: in the median scenario the cap matches the volume and value that replanned \scen{NDY} delivers, so the gap between the cap and the announced \scen{NDY} level (11\% on landbase~1 at 4\%) is mostly the gap between an announced and a deliverable flow, not a loss relative to replanning; individual scenarios differ widely (on landbase~1 at 4\%, NPV is 6\% lower). The institution rerun and E4 qualify precommitment: binding each replan to the realized harvest and the original terminal date makes the plan consistent, but a fixed terminal date shortens the horizon of every later plan; carrying the flow history alone leaves a quarter of the scenarios inconsistent, almost all at a zero rate, and a rolling-mean floor anchored to realized harvest must be loosened, if usually by about 1\%, in two thirds of the replans. Stronger intertemporal commitment can therefore improve consistency but cannot guarantee feasibility.

\hypertarget{limitations}{%
\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}}

Several limitations define the scope of these claims. First, the thesis does not report its base-case schedule, so validation relies on its printed anchors (Section~\ref{case-study-data}), and the landbases are reconstructions of its verbal descriptions. Second, the reproduction uses Model~I where the thesis used Model~II, because \texttt{ws3} does not yet compile Model~II flow constraints; their behavioral equivalence under sequential replanning remains to be demonstrated (the null test checks the Model~I rebuild, not the substitution). Third, a harvested stand regenerates under a fixed prescription, and the objective has no treatment costs or commercial thinnings, so two channels of the thesis cannot arise here: the choice of management intensity at regeneration, which its combination sets varied \citep[pp.~74, 81]{daugherty1991credibility}, and the inconsistency it found without flow constraints when a later planner ended a thinning regime early \citep[p.~123]{daugherty1991credibility}. Fourth, our terminal constraints follow the thesis's definitions and Table~5.3 rotations but use our fixed regeneration prescriptions; with the model's own highest-PNV rotations (shorter, longer for CM-CE), flow-constrained occurrence is 45\% rather than 49\% (the same classification in 331 of 360 scenarios) but mean magnitude 0.077 rather than 0.072. The constraints remove end-of-horizon liquidation, but end periods still carry on average 53\% of a scenario's summed divergence (56\% occurrence over all fifteen periods), so we report the thesis's window. Fifth, this is a single case study: the 49\% occurrence is a property of this scenario grid, its 5\% tolerance (26--68\% at 10--2\%), and, for the random landbases, the draw (36--41\%), not an estimate of prevalence. The reproduction's magnitudes are below the thesis's on its own measure and agree with its per-landbase pattern only in part, which may reflect the reconstructed landbases, the fixed regeneration prescriptions, and the escalation of net values. Sixth, among the extensions, the E2 caps depend on the even-flow acceptance thresholds, whose influence we did not test; E4's rolling-mean rows rely on pinned \texttt{ws3} constraint internals (regression-tested to reduce exactly to \scen{NDY}); and E1 does not test calendar-fixed declining schedules.
